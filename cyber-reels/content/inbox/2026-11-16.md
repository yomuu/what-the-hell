# Incident decks

## Deck 1

- id: inc-qubole
- kind: incident
- title: Qubole cross-account trust abuse
- tag (year): 2026
- region: Global (cloud/AWS)
- status: Developing. How the attacker got the Qubole-side credentials hasn't been disclosed.
- attack (suggested): T1199 Trusted Relationship; T1078.004 Valid Accounts: Cloud Accounts; T1530 Data from Cloud Storage

Cards:

1. What happened
   From 14 to 26 October 2026, an attacker used credentials tied to a Qubole AWS account to get into the AWS accounts of many Qubole customers. Wiz published the findings on 9 November 2026, working with Sentra and Mandiant.
   Sources: [W1], [B1], [H1]

2. How they got in
   Customers had created IAM roles that trusted Qubole's AWS account, so Qubole's data platform could reach their data. The attacker took over two Qubole IAM users and assumed those roles. In many cases the roles were left over from integrations the customer no longer used.
   Sources: [W1], [H1], [C1]

3. Impact
   The attacker read secrets from Secrets Manager, SSM Parameter Store and Lambda environment variables, then tested the keys it found. It also copied S3 data, sometimes whole buckets. BleepingComputer reports tens of companies affected. Some victims had no S3 data events or access logs, so they couldn't tell which files were read.
   Sources: [W1], [B1], [C1]

4. Lessons
   Remove vendor roles you no longer use. Trust the vendor's specific IAM principal, not their whole account (":root"), and add source IP or VPC endpoint conditions. Turn on S3 data events for sensitive buckets. Don't treat the External ID as a secret.
   Sources: [W1], [C1]

Maps: A01 Broken Access Control (over-broad, stale cross-account trust). Secondary: A09 Security Logging and Alerting Failures (missing S3 data events). NIST CSF 2.0: PR.AA (Identity Management, Authentication and Access Control) and GV.SC (Supply Chain Risk Management).

Check question:
- Q: Why did the attacker's requests into customer AWS accounts succeed?
- A (correct): The customers' roles still trusted Qubole's AWS account
- B: The customers' S3 buckets were set to public
- C: AWS itself was breached
- Explanation: No AWS flaw was needed. Stale trust policies let anyone holding Qubole-side credentials assume the roles.
- Sources: [W1], [H1]

Not on the cards (UNCONFIRMED):
- Claim of responsibility by an actor called "n0n". Wiz's 11 Nov update cites Risky Business, but the claim itself isn't verified. [W1]
- Claim that Qubole's setup guide grants read access to all buckets. Single DEV Community post only.
- Sentra's "millions of files" from one customer. Reported via BleepingComputer, but there's no exact figure. [B1]

## Deck 2

- id: inc-sshole
- kind: incident
- title: SSHole: OpenSSH password bypass
- tag (year): 2026
- region: Global
- status: Confirmed and actively exploited (CISA KEV, 9 November 2026)
- attack (suggested): T1190 Exploit Public-Facing Application; T1133 External Remote Services

Cards:

1. What happened
   On 5 November 2026 OpenSSH released 10.6p2 to fix CVE-2026-123158, nicknamed "SSHole". On 9 November CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue.
   Sources: [K1], [K2], [S1], [B2]

2. How they get in
   On Linux systems that use the musl C library with PAM turned off, a very long password could get through SSH password login. Alpine Linux is the main example. Distros built on glibc, like Debian, Ubuntu, Red Hat and SUSE, aren't affected, according to HKCERT.
   Sources: [HK1], [AL1], [S1]
   Reviewer note: sources disagree on the exact password length (256 or 257 to 1,024 characters), so the card just says "very long".

3. Impact
   Exploitation started fast. SANS ISC logged scans on 5 November, the same day a proof of concept appeared. BleepingComputer and SecurityWeek report successful break-ins after that. The CVE is rated CVSS 8.1 (High).
   Sources: [ISC1], [B2], [S2]

4. Lessons
   Patch internet-facing SSH fast once a flaw hits the KEV list. Use key-based logins and set PasswordAuthentication to no. Know which of your hosts and containers run Alpine, because a "which OS?" inventory decides whether you're exposed.
   Sources: [AL1], [K2], [S1]

Maps: A07 Authentication Failures. NIST CSF 2.0: ID.RA (Risk Assessment, vulnerability tracking) and PR.PS (Platform Security, patching and secure configuration).

Check question:
- Q: Which setting blocks SSHole even before you patch?
- A (correct): PasswordAuthentication no in sshd_config
- B: Changing SSH from port 22 to 2222
- C: Turning on PermitRootLogin yes
- Explanation: The bypass only works on password logins. Moving the port only hides it from lazy scans, and allowing root login makes things worse.
- Sources: [AL1], [B2]

Not on the cards (UNCONFIRMED):
- An aggregator says CISA's KEV entry title calls the weakness "unexpected status code or return value". If true, that also fits A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions. Please confirm on [K1] before adding A10.
- Patched Alpine packages won't show "10.6p2" in their version string (for example openssh-server 10.3_p1-r2 on Alpine 3.24). This is from a Medium post plus a secondary blog. Check the Alpine tracker [AL1].

## Deck 3

- id: inc-amadeus
- kind: incident
- title: Amadeus airline booking data (Qantas)
- tag (year): 2026
- region: AU (Qantas affected; covered by NZ Herald)
- status: Developing. Qantas hasn't confirmed whether any of its customers were affected.
- attack (suggested): T1199 Trusted Relationship; T1078 Valid Accounts

Cards:

1. What happened
   In early November 2026 Qantas said travel technology provider Amadeus had told it about unauthorised access to one of Amadeus's systems. Other airlines, including Cathay Pacific, also disclosed impacts. The access window reportedly ran from August 2023 to September 2026.
   Sources: [Q1], [CD1], [NZH1], [ACS1]

2. How they got in
   Amadeus says a vulnerability in a booking-display environment made certain booking data accessible. Cathay Pacific says Amadeus told it that the attacker used login credentials assigned to a system account. Neither company has published full technical details.
   Sources: [ACS1], [CD1], [CX via secondary: TCE1]

3. Impact
   Cathay Pacific says about 53,000 bookings were affected. Qantas says its own systems weren't affected and it doesn't yet know which of its customers, if any, had data accessed. Amadeus says it has no evidence that payment or financial data was accessed.
   Sources: [Q1], [CD1], [NZH1], [TCE1]

4. Lessons
   Your customers' data is only as safe as your supplier's systems. Service and system accounts need the same care as human ones: strong secrets, rotation, monitoring and least privilege. A breach that runs for three years undetected points to a monitoring gap.
   Sources: general lesson drawn from [ACS1] and [CD1]; the 3-year window is from [ACS1] and [CD1]

Maps: NIST CSF 2.0 GV.SC (Supply Chain Risk Management) and DE.CM (Continuous Monitoring). OWASP: A07 Authentication Failures (system account credentials), pending confirmation of the cause.

Check question:
- Q: Qantas says its own systems weren't touched. Why might its customers still be affected?
- A (correct): Their booking data was held in a supplier's system
- B: Qantas staff reused passwords
- C: Customers clicked a phishing link
- Explanation: Third-party risk means your data can leak from someone else's network. Only the Amadeus side has been reported as accessed.
- Sources: [Q1], [CD1]

Not on the cards (UNCONFIRMED or conflicting):
- JAL figures: sources report about 90,000 and about 92,000, with a separate 36,100 figure. Left out.
- The SAS figure of 350,164 records comes from one regulatory-filing report and one outlet calls it unverified. Left out.
- Qantas customer count: not stated by any source.
- Sources don't agree on the exact day Qantas first commented. Cards just say "early November 2026".

- tag (year): 2026
- region: Global
- status: Developing. Maersk's statement and the WhiteArmor gang's claims haven't been linked by Maersk.
- attack (suggested): T1486 not confirmed; use T1567 Exfiltration Over Web Service only if confirmed. Suggest leaving the attack field empty for now.

Cards:

1. What happened
   Around 11 November 2026, shipping company Maersk confirmed unauthorised access to and data extraction from a local legacy system it had inherited through an earlier acquisition. The WhiteArmor ransomware gang listed Maersk on its leak site.
   Sources: [B3], [SMN1], [SA1]

2. How they got in
   Not disclosed. Maersk hasn't said how the attackers got in or when the intrusion began. Reporting by Cybernews and Security Affairs links the data to LF Logistics, which Maersk bought in 2022, but Maersk hasn't confirmed this.

## Deck 5

- id: inc-fakejob-android
- kind: incident
- title: Fake job app malware (NCSC NZ warning)
- tag (year): 2026
- region: NZ
- status: Confirmed by an official source (NCSC NZ, 4 November 2026). The app and victim numbers aren't public.
- attack (suggested): T1204 User Execution; T1566 Phishing (lure via social media and messaging apps)

Cards:

1. What happened
   On 4 November 2026 NCSC New Zealand warned that malicious actors are spreading Android malware disguised as a job-seeking app. It can also be disguised as a banking app.
   Sources: [N1], [OYO1], [NW1]

2. How they get in
   Victims are tricked into sideloading the app, meaning installing it from outside the Play Store. It then asks for a series of settings, especially Accessibility access. NCSC's Q2 2026 report describes the same pattern: fake jobs advertised on social media, then a move to a messaging app and a link to download another app.
   Sources: [N1], [OYO1], [N2]

3. Impact
   NCSC hasn't named the app or said how many New Zealanders were affected. It warns that uninstalling the app may not fully remove the malware. A factory reset is more likely to work.
   Sources: [N1], [NW1]

4. Lessons
   Never enable installs from unknown sources. A real employer won't need you to install an app from a link. Be very wary of any app asking for Accessibility access, because that permission can let it read and control your screen.
   Sources: [N1], [OYO1]

Maps: NIST CSF 2.0 PR.AT (Awareness and Training) and PR.PS (Platform Security: device settings). OWASP: not applicable (user-side malware).

Check question:
- Q: A recruiter on social media asks you to install their "jobs app" from a link. What's the safest move?
- A (correct): Decline and check the company through its official website
- B: Install it but deny the first permission it asks for
- C: Install it, then uninstall if it looks odd
- Explanation: NCSC warns that uninstalling may not remove the malware. The safe move is not to sideload it at all.
- Sources: [N1], [OYO1]

# Quick-fire cards (one card each)

1. Heading: Check your SSH login setting
   Text: Run "sudo sshd -T | grep -i passwordauthentication" to see the setting sshd is actually using. "passwordauthentication no" blocks SSHole-style password bypasses.
   Sources: [AL1], [B2]. The sshd -T flag (print the effective config) is standard OpenSSH behaviour; see the sshd(8) man page at https://man.openbsd.org/sshd

2. Heading: Spot the mistake: a trust policy
   Text: A role trusts "arn:aws:iam::<vendor-account>:root" with no conditions. The mistake is that it trusts the vendor's whole account. Name the vendor's exact role, add source IP or VPC endpoint conditions, and delete the role once you stop using the vendor.
   Sources: [W1], [C1]

3. Heading: S3 reads aren't logged by default
   Text: CloudTrail records management events by default, but S3 object reads are "data events" you must turn on. In the Qubole attack, some victims couldn't tell which files were taken.
   Sources: [W1] for the Qubole gap; AWS docs: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/awscloudtrail/latest/userguide/logging-data-events-with-cloudtrail.html

4. Heading: NZ by the numbers (2025/26)
   Text: NCSC NZ logged 4,673 reports in 2025/26. It flagged 369 incidents of potential national significance (up from 331) and recorded 4 "highly significant" C2 incidents. That's as many as in the previous ten years combined.
   Sources: [N3], [N4], [NZH2]
   Reviewer note: the reported direct losses of 23.8 million (down from 26.9 million) are left off the card because the currency wasn't confirmed in the summaries. It's almost certainly NZD; check [N3].

5. Heading: KEV means "it's being used now"
   Text: CISA's KEV catalogue lists flaws with evidence of real-world exploitation. US federal agencies get fixed deadlines, and SSHole's was 12 November, just 3 days after it was added. Everyone else should treat KEV as a "patch first" list.
   Sources: [K1], [K2], [ES1]

# Ideas for future weeks

1. Third-party and vendor access reviews: a deck on finding and removing stale SaaS integrations, cross-account roles and supplier accounts. Why: Qubole, Amadeus and Maersk this week were all third-party or inherited-system breaches, and "how would you manage vendor risk?" is a common cloud security interview question.

2. OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2026) and agentic AI risks: a short OWASP-style series on prompt injection and over-permissive AI agents. Why: NCSC NZ's Cyber Threat Report 2026 centres on AI-driven threats, and employers increasingly expect entry-level candidates to discuss AI security basics.

3. Recruitment scams from both sides: fake job offers that push malware, and fake candidates trying to get hired for insider access. Why: it's directly relevant while job hunting, and it builds the social engineering judgement SOC and IT security roles test in interviews.
