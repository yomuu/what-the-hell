## Deck 1

- id: inc-ivanti-ics-chain
- kind: incident
- title: Ivanti VPN zero-day chain
- tag (year): 2026
- region: (none)
- status: Confirmed (CISA KEV, 13 and 30 October 2026). Victim numbers haven't been published.
- attack (suggested): T1190 Exploit Public-Facing Application; T1070 Indicator Removal (log entries wiped)

Cards:

1. What happened
   In early October 2026, Volexity caught an actor it tracks as UTA0576 attacking Ivanti Connect Secure VPN appliances with zero-days. Ivanti patched the first flaw, CVE-2026-104935, on 13 October. It patched two more, CVE-2026-111357 and CVE-2026-118527, on 29 October.
   Sources: [CI2], [CI1], [HNS1], [SW1]

2. How they got in
   A crafted web request got past the login without an account and led to code execution (CVE-2026-104935). The two later flaws let the attacker get root. The victim's appliance was fully patched at the time, so this was a true zero-day chain.
   Sources: [CI1], [HNS1], [BC1], [CSO1]

3. Impact
   The attacker used the appliance's own Active Directory service account to look for LAPS admin passwords and BitLocker recovery keys. It left no web shell. Instead it reran the exploit chain for each command, in memory, and wiped matching log entries. No victim count has been published.
   Sources: [HNS1], [SA1], [CSA1]
   Reviewer note: the LAPS, BitLocker and log-wiping details come from secondary write-ups of Volexity's 13 October post [V1], which I couldn't open. They agree across [HNS1], [SA1] and [CSA1].

4. Lessons
   Patch to 25.1.1.3 or 22.8R2.6 or later, because systems patched on 13 October were still exposed to the later two flaws. CISA also says to reset credentials and certificates. Send logs off the appliance to a SIEM, since on-box logs can be wiped. Give the VPN's service account only the AD access it needs.
   Sources: [CI1], [SW1], [BC1]. The SIEM and least-privilege points are general lessons drawn from the log wiping and AD queries in [HNS1].

Maps: A07 Authentication Failures (login bypassed without an account). NIST CSF 2.0: PR.PS (Platform Security: patching), DE.CM (Continuous Monitoring) and ID.RA (Risk Assessment: tracking KEV).

Check question:
- Q: Why might Ivanti's Integrity Checker Tool show nothing on a hacked appliance?
- A (correct): The attacker's commands ran in memory and left no changed files
- B: The attacker uninstalled the checker
- C: VPN appliances can't be scanned at all
- Explanation: CISA warns the tool may miss this exploitation because the commands execute in memory. Memory and network forensics are needed.
- Sources: [CI1], [HNS1]

Not on the cards (UNCONFIRMED or conflicting):
- Affected and fixed versions differ between sources. One CISA-based account adds 25.1.2.2 and earlier, and another names 25.1.3.1 as an upgrade target. The card uses the versions SecurityWeek and BleepingComputer agree on. Check Ivanti's advisory before publishing.
- Sources give 1 or 3 October as the date exploitation started. The card just says "early October".
- CVSS scores: Ivanti rates CVE-2026-111357 8.8 and CVE-2026-118527 4.3. NVD lists 7.0 for CVE-2026-111357. Scores are left off the cards.
- One write-up says the attacker also used a custom Kerberos tool and password spraying. That's a single source.

## Deck 2

- id: inc-fireworks-ai
- kind: incident
- title: Fireworks AI breach via a cloud provider
- tag (year): 2026
- region: (none; cloud)
- status: Developing. Fireworks hasn't named the cloud provider or said how many customers were affected. Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 is investigating.
- attack (suggested): T1199 Trusted Relationship. Leave further techniques out until a root cause is published.

Cards:

1. What happened
   AI model platform Fireworks AI first identified a security incident on 6 October 2026. In a 9 November update, it said an attacker had also reached its development machines on 24 October.
   Sources: [TR1], [BC2], [REG1]

2. How they got in
   A third-party cloud provider that hosted Fireworks' research and development machines had its own security incident on 23 October. The attacker used that access to reach Fireworks' development machines and run an unauthorised workload there. Fireworks hasn't named the provider.
   Sources: [TR1], [BC2]

3. Impact
   The attacker reached customer account and billing information. For a limited set of accounts, it also reached stored secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights. Payment card numbers weren't exposed because Fireworks doesn't store them. Fireworks says it hasn't detected any impact on its Inference, Training SDK or Nexus products.
   Sources: [TR1], [BC2], [REG1]

4. Lessons
   Your supplier's suppliers are part of your attack surface too. Keep development machines away from customer secrets and data. If a provider says secrets may be exposed, rotate every key you stored there straight away, rather than waiting to hear whether your account was affected.
   Sources: general lessons drawn from [TR1] and [BC2]. The advice to rotate secrets is repeated in commentary such as [BERI1].

Maps: NIST CSF 2.0 GV.SC (Supply Chain Risk Management), PR.DS (Data Security) and PR.AA (Identity Management, Authentication and Access Control: secrets). OWASP: no category mapped, because the root cause isn't public.

Check question:
- Q: A provider says some customers' stored secrets were accessed. You kept an API key there. What's your first move?
- A (correct): Rotate the key now
- B: Wait until they confirm your account was affected
- C: Delete the old key from your own notes
- Explanation: Assume a key is exposed once its storage location is breached. Rotation makes a stolen key useless.
- Sources: [TR1], [BERI1]

Not on the cards (UNCONFIRMED):
- One summary says the 6 October incident involved misused internal credentials. I couldn't tell which outlet said this, so it's left out.
- The Record reports that Fireworks suspects links to criminals involved in "LLMjacking" (reselling stolen access to AI models). This is a single outlet reporting a suspicion, so it's left out. It's suggested as a future idea instead.
- The Information reports that some large customers are considering other providers. That's single-source.
- Some outlets call 24 October a "second hack", while Fireworks presents it as part of its October incident. The cards avoid the label.

## Deck 3

- id: inc-canvas-instructure
- kind: incident
- title: Canvas (Instructure) breach hits NZ and AU universities
- tag (year): 2026
- region: NZ/AU
- status: Confirmed by Instructure and affected universities. Instructure hasn't published a full root cause report.
- attack (suggested): T1491.002 External Defacement (login pages changed on 7 May). Leave initial access empty until a root cause is published.

Cards:

1. What happened
   In late April and early May 2026, the ShinyHunters group breached Instructure, which makes the Canvas learning platform. On 7 May it changed Canvas login pages at about 330 institutions. The University of Auckland, AUT and Victoria University of Wellington were among those affected.
   Sources: [NZH1], [RNZ1], [TC1], [UA1]

2. How they got in
   Instructure said the attacker exploited an issue related to its Free-For-Teacher accounts. These let educators sign up without being verified by a school. Instructure hasn't published technical details, and it has shut the free program down.
   Sources: [CYI1], [RR1], [BD1]

3. Impact
   Instructure said the exposed data likely included names, email addresses, student ID numbers and messages. It said passwords, birthdates, government IDs and financial details weren't believed to be involved. Canvas went offline during assessments, and NZ universities gave students extensions.
   Sources: [NZH1], [RNZ1], [STF1], [UA1]

4. Lessons
   The universities' own systems weren't breached, but their students' data still was, because it sat with a supplier. A free, unverified sign-up tier is attack surface. On 12 May Instructure said it had reached an agreement with the hackers, but it admitted there's no way to be sure the data was erased.
   Sources: [UA1], [TC1], [AP1]

Maps: NIST CSF 2.0 GV.SC (Supply Chain Risk Management) and RC.CO (Incident Recovery Communication). OWASP: tentatively A06 Insecure Design (open, unverified sign-ups reportedly ran on production infrastructure). The cause isn't confirmed, so treat this mapping as tentative.

Check question:
- Q: Instructure says the hackers sent "shred logs" showing stolen data was destroyed. What's the safe takeaway?
- A (correct): Treat the data as exposed anyway
- B: The data is now safe, so no action is needed
- C: It proves the universities' own servers were hacked
- Explanation: Instructure itself said there's no way to be sure the data was erased. You can't verify a criminal's promise.
- Sources: [AP1], [TC1]

Reviewer notes:
- The OAIC issued a statement [OAIC1] on how complaints work: public universities are generally exempt from the Privacy Act, and complaints go first to Instructure or the institution. This could be a "law" link card. Please open [OAIC1] to confirm the wording.
- The University of Canberra says 25 Australian and New Zealand universities were affected [UC1]. That's an official university source, so it could be added to card 1 if there's room.

Not on the cards (UNCONFIRMED or conflicting):
- The cross-site scripting (XSS) explanation for the Free-For-Teacher issue comes from vendor advisories. Instructure hasn't confirmed it.
- Data volume: the hackers claimed 3.5 or 3.65 TB (figures differ). Claims of about 275 million users and about 8,800 to 9,000 institutions come from attackers or secondary reporting.
- Whether a ransom was paid: Instructure hasn't said. A former FBI official said a payment was likely. That's an opinion, so it's left out.
- The detection date is given as 29 April, 30 April or 1 May depending on the source. The card says "late April and early May".

## Deck 4

- id: inc-dprk-itworker-nz
- kind: incident
- title: North Korean IT worker hired by an NZ business
- tag (year): 2026
- region: NZ
- status: Confirmed by an official source (NCSC NZ Cyber Threat Report 2026, released 24 September 2026). The business isn't named.
- attack (suggested): T1078 Valid Accounts (access through legitimately issued work credentials). Optional, so it could be left empty.

Cards:

1. What happened
   On 24 September 2026, NCSC NZ's Cyber Threat Report 2026 described a large New Zealand business that hired a remote IT contractor who turned out to be a North Korean operative. The business hasn't been named.
   Sources: [N1], [N2], [1N1], [RNZ2]

2. How they got in
   They got in through hiring, not hacking. The worker used fake identity documents and a New Zealand address as a contact point. A New Zealand citizen was recruited to receive and operate the company's laptop.
   Sources: [N2], [1N1], [NZH2]

3. Impact
   The business grew suspicious and contacted NCSC and NZ Police, then ended the contract and refused to pay. The worker then claimed to hold commercially sensitive information and threatened to release it unless paid. NCSC says this activity also breaches UN sanctions, which apply under NZ law.
   Sources: [N2], [1N1], [RNZ2]

4. Lessons
   Identity checks are a security control. NCSC suggests interviewing candidates face to face and having new staff pick up IT equipment in person. Red flags include wanting payment in cryptocurrency, refusing video calls and unusual working hours.
   Sources: [N1], [RNZ2], [HCA1]

Maps: NIST CSF 2.0 GV.RR (Roles, Responsibilities and Authorities: security in HR practices) and PR.AA (Identity Management, Authentication and Access Control: identity proofing). OWASP: not applicable (insider and hiring risk).

Check question:
- Q: Which NCSC NZ tip most directly stops someone else using a new hire's work laptop?
- A (correct): Make new staff collect IT equipment in person
- B: Require longer passwords on the laptop
- C: Send the laptop by tracked courier
- Explanation: In this case a local accomplice received and ran the laptop. A courier delivers to whatever address you're given, and a strong password doesn't help if the wrong person holds it.
- Sources: [N2], [1N1]

Reviewer note: this deck pairs well with inc-fakejob-android, since it shows the other side of recruitment fraud. It's also a likely interview topic.

Not on the cards:
- RNZ reports that police won't say what action they've taken against the worker or the NZ citizen who helped. That's accurate, but it's left off to keep the cards short.
- In July 2026 NZ joined a multi-country warning about North Korean IT workers. This was mentioned in coverage, but I couldn't find the advisory's own page, so it's left out.

# Quick-fire cards (one card each)

1. Heading: Port 445 and the Outlook zero-day
   Text: On 10 November Microsoft fixed an exploited Outlook flaw, CVE-2026-124194, that can leak NTLM password hashes. Blocking outbound TCP 445 (SMB) to the internet cuts off a common path for these leaks. Patching Office is still the fix.
   Sources: [MS1], [CSO2], [DR1]. Blocking outbound TCP 445 as an NTLM-leak defence is long-standing general guidance.
   Reviewer note: sources say installing the Windows cumulative update alone doesn't fix this bug, because it ships in the Office updates. Consider adding that if there's room.

2. Heading: Spot the mistake: "We patched Ivanti in October"
   Text: "We installed Ivanti's 13 October fix, so we're safe." The mistake is that two more chained flaws were patched on 29 October. Builds 22.8R2.5 and 25.1.1.2 are still exposed. One round of patching isn't the finish line.
   Sources: [BC1], [SW1], [CI1]

3. Heading: A clean scan isn't proof
   Text: In the Ivanti attacks, commands ran in memory and log entries were wiped. CISA warned that Ivanti's Integrity Checker Tool may not detect it. Investigators had to use memory images and crash dumps.
   Sources: [CI1], [HNS1]

4. Heading: Fake candidate red flags
   Text: NCSC NZ red flags for remote hires include wanting payment in cryptocurrency, refusing video calls, odd working hours, and a laptop that goes to someone else.
   Sources: [N2], [RNZ2], [HCA1]

5. Heading: AU breach reports (Jan to Jun 2026)
   Text: The OAIC received 667 notifiable data breach notifications in the first half of 2026, up from 662. Breaches affecting more than a million people rose from 4 to 11.
   Sources: [IBA1], [CD1]
   Reviewer note: these come from trade press summaries of the OAIC's report. Please confirm against the OAIC's Notifiable Data Breaches report on oaic.gov.au before publishing, or drop this card.

# Ideas for future weeks

1. LLMjacking and AI platform security: how stolen cloud and API keys get used to resell AI model access, and how to spot it in billing and logs. Why: Fireworks AI and other AI platforms disclosed breaches in October, and cloud security roles increasingly cover AI workloads.

2. Edge devices under attack: why VPNs and firewalls are a top way in, why on-box logs can't be trusted, and what "forward logs to a SIEM" means in practice. Why: Ivanti-style zero-day chains keep happening, and SOC and infrastructure security interviews often ask how you'd investigate a compromised appliance.

3. NTLM in plain language: what an NTLM hash is, how relay and pass-the-hash work, and why SMB signing and blocking outbound port 445 help. Why: this week's Outlook zero-day targets NTLM hashes, and Active Directory attack basics come up in almost every SOC analyst interview.
